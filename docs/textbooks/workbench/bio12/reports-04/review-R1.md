## Flags, most serious first
Format: where | says now | wrong | fix [NEW] | kind | source | confidence.

1. Topic 04, Crack the MCQ Q2 (book p. 190), option (c). Says: label "two alleles of one gene", nudge "(c) is multiple allelism." Two alleles of one gene is ordinary allelism; multiple allelism needs more than two alleles. A student is taught a wrong definition. Fix [NEW] nudge: "<b>(c)</b> describes ordinary allelism (two alleles of one gene); multiple allelism needs more than two alleles of the gene in the population." Answer (a) stays. Kind: unclear/misleading biology. Source: book. Certain.

2. Topic 04, definition "Polygenic inheritance" ("three or more genes"), Practice 1 answer ("three or more additive genes"), against Worked example 4 and Practice 5 (a two-gene model called polygenic). Internal contradiction; the usual definition is "two or more" (NCERT: many). Fix [NEW] def: "a mode of inheritance in which a single trait is controlled by two or more genes (usually several), each contributing a small additive effect." Practice 1: "A trait controlled by two or more (usually several) additive genes; e.g. human skin colour (or height)". Example 4 stem: add "(a two-gene model)". Kind: biology/internal inconsistency. Source: book. Certain.

3. Topic 02, Key definitions "Linkage group" ("all the genes on a single chromosome, inherited as a unit"; "human 23"), Procedure F, Example 2 trap ("Always halve to the haploid number"), Pitfall 1, cheat sheet ("human 23"; "pea 7, Drosophila 4, maize 10, human 23"). "Inherited as a unit" is wrong (linkage is usually incomplete, the topic's own crossing-over). Linkage groups = n only when sex chromosomes are alike or counted as one pair; a human male has 24 (22 + X + Y), a female 23. Fix [NEW] def: "Linkage group: the genes on one chromosome, which tend to be inherited together. The number of linkage groups equals the haploid number <i>n</i> when the sex chromosomes are alike or counted as one pair (pea 7, <i>Drosophila</i> 4, maize 10); where X and Y differ and are counted as two groups there is one more (a human male has 22 + X + Y = 24, a female 23)." Example 2 trap, last sentence: "Halve 2<i>n</i> to the haploid number (the chapter's rule for a plant with no distinct sex chromosomes)." Cheat sheet: "human 23 (24 in a male, since X and Y differ)". Kind: biology. Source: book. Certain (the rule), likely (how far to qualify).

4. Topic 02, defs "Complete linkage: no recombinants formed (genes never separate)" and Procedure B "at or beyond that, the genes assort as if they were on different chromosomes entirely". "Never" fails: complete linkage is the rare limiting case (male Drosophila shows it because males have no crossing over). Fix [NEW]: "Complete linkage: no recombinants formed (rare, as in male <i>Drosophila</i>, where crossing over does not occur)." Procedure B last sentence: "The ceiling is <b>50%</b>: at 50% the genes behave as if they were on different chromosomes." Kind: biology. Source: book. Likely.

5. Topic 01, Key definitions "Recessive allele: expresses only in the homozygous state" and "Law of Dominance: in a heterozygote, only the dominant allele expresses" (also Section 3 and cheat sheet "Three laws"). Fails for X-linked recessive alleles in the hemizygous male (the chapter's own Topic 02 and 03) and under incomplete dominance or codominance (the same topic). Fix [NEW]: "Recessive allele: expresses in the homozygous state (and in the hemizygous male for an X-linked gene); masked in the heterozygote under complete dominance." "Law of Dominance: with complete dominance, in a heterozygote only the dominant allele expresses; the recessive is masked." Cheat sheet: "Dominance (with complete dominance, F<sub>1</sub> shows the dominant; explains 3 ∶ 1)". Kind: biology. Source: book. Certain.

6. Topic 02, Pitfall 4 "Recombination frequency can never exceed 50%... A '60% recombination' option is always wrong"; also cheat sheet "ceiling 50%", Topic 02 defs "1 cM = 1% RF". True for two genes in one test cross; but the same topic equates RF with map distance, and map distances built by adding short intervals exceed 50 cM. Fix [NEW] Pitfall 4: "<b>Forgetting the 50% recombination ceiling.</b> The recombination frequency between two genes in a test cross cannot exceed 50% (a crossover involves only two of the four chromatids); genes showing 50% behave as if unlinked. A '60% recombination' option for two genes is wrong. (Long map distances, built by adding short intervals, can exceed 50 cM.)" Kind: biology. Source: book. Likely.

7. Topic 02, Procedure C step 3 "Sanity check: middle-gene distances should add up to the outer distance." Unqualified; additivity holds for short distances only (double crossovers). The book qualifies it only in the Pro-Tip and Additional F. Fix [NEW]: "Sanity check: for short distances the two shorter distances add up to the outer distance (over long stretches double crossovers make the measured values fall short)." The "Why" line: "on a straight line the two end-points are the farthest apart, and the two short segments sum to the long one (for short distances)." Kind: biology. Source: book. Certain.

8. Topic 05, Limiting (2) "Only mutations in the germ line... are passed to offspring: somatic mutations affect only the individual and are not inherited", Somatic vs germinal def, Pitfall 4, cheat sheet "only germinal mutations are passed on". Somatic mutations pass to all daughter cells (cancer, clones; bud sports in vegetative propagation). Fix [NEW]: "Only mutations in the germ line (gamete-forming cells) are passed to the offspring of sexually reproducing organisms; a somatic mutation stays in the individual, and in the cells descended from the mutated cell (as in cancer), but is not passed to offspring." Pitfall 4 bold: "Forgetting that somatic mutations are not passed to offspring." Kind: biology. Source: book. Likely.

9. Topic 04, concept "This additive logic was first explored by Galton (1883)" and cheat sheet "Galton." Doubtful attribution; the additive multiple-factor explanation is Nilsson-Ehle (1909, wheat), applied to human skin colour by Davenport (1910s); Galton (1880s) studied continuous variation. Fix [NEW]: delete the Galton sentence and the cheat-sheet "Galton."; keep "because the resulting trait can be measured on a scale, it is also called <b>quantitative inheritance</b>." (If an attribution is wanted: "The multiple-factor explanation of continuous traits was worked out by Nilsson-Ehle (1909) and applied to human skin colour by Davenport.") Kind: attribution. Source: book. Likely (medium-high).

10. Topic 02, defs "Coupling and repulsion": "in repulsion, a dominant and a recessive arrive together and tend to remain apart from the other dominant" is muddled. Fix [NEW]: "In <b>coupling</b>, the two dominant alleles lie on the same chromosome (<i>AB</i>/<i>ab</i>) and tend to stay together in a gamete; in <b>repulsion</b>, each chromosome carries one dominant and one recessive allele (<i>Ab</i>/<i>aB</i>), so the two dominant alleles come from different parents and tend to stay apart." Kind: unclear/misleading. Source: book. Certain it is unclear.

11. Topic 03, Concept "The catch is that most are recessive: you need two faulty copies". Many are dominant (Huntington disease, Marfan syndrome, achondroplasia). Fix [NEW]: "The catch is that many are recessive (others, such as Huntington disease, are dominant): a recessive disorder needs two faulty copies." Kind: biology. Source: book. Likely.

12. Topic 01, Pro-Tip "never draw a giant Punnett square" and cheat sheet "Gamete types = 2^n". Only for independently assorting (unlinked) genes; linked genes give unequal gamete classes (Topic 02). Fix [NEW] Pro-Tip: "For any multi-gene cross with independently assorting (unlinked) genes, never draw a giant Punnett square." Cheat sheet: "Gamete types = 2<sup><i>n</i></sup> for independently assorting genes, where <i>n</i> = number of heterozygous gene pairs." Also Procedure B "yields the classic 9 ∶ 3 ∶ 3 ∶ 1" add "for unlinked genes". Kind: biology. Source: book. Likely.

## Overall judgement
Topics 01 to 05 are a faithful, arithmetically sound transcription; every cross, probability, ratio and count is right, all marked MCQ answers are right, and there is no figure error because there is no figure. The book's weaknesses are the usual ones: unqualified rules (recessive "only homozygous", "inherited as a unit", "never exceed 50%", additivity of map distances, "only germline", "most disorders recessive", "never clean ratios"), unsupported exam-frequency superlatives, one doubtful attribution (Galton 1883), and one mis-explained MCQ distractor (Topic 04 Q2 (c)); the one transcription-level inconsistency is the upright genotypes in Topic 04. Fixing flags 1 to 8 and 22a removes everything a teacher would call wrong; the rest are qualifications. The key points already hold the corrected versions, so the chapter text should be brought into line with them.
