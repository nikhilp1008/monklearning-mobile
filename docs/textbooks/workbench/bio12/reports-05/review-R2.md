Wrong: they are two different properties and the code is both (Q5 in the same topic keys (c) "non-ambiguous, degenerate"). The opposite of unambiguous is ambiguous. The text contradicts the book's own answer. Fix [NEW]: "Degenerate = many codons → one amino acid; unambiguous = each codon → only one amino acid. They are different properties and the genetic code has both." Also change the row label to "Degenerate and unambiguous are not opposites". Kind: biology error. Source: book. Certain. (Key point topic 12 already says "degenerate and unambiguous".)

3. **C, Practice 5(b) and its answer** (p. 279; the F answer 5 does not repeat it). Says: "(b) Constitutive: expression is higher than in (a) and does not depend on lactose". 4. **G, Traps row "Chargaff applies to RNA"** (p. 289). Says: "holds only for double-stranded DNA; single-stranded nucleic acids do not obey it." Double-stranded RNA also pairs A with U, so the 'Chargaff-style rule for RNA' is true for double-stranded RNA; the trap is the A = T wording. Fix [NEW] correct-fact cell: "Chargaff's rules (A = T, G = C) hold for double-stranded DNA. Single-stranded nucleic acids (single-stranded DNA, most RNA) do not obey them; only a double-stranded RNA pairs A with U." Kind: overgeneral. Source: book. Likely. (The key point already says this.)

5. **E, probability claims** (pp. 282, 283).
 a. Method: "(1/100)^5 = 1/10^10: astronomically small, making forensic identification essentially certain." 10^-10 × about 8 × 10^9 people = about 0.8 expected matching people, and loci are never perfectly independent. Fix [NEW]: "(1/100)^5 = 1/10^10: very small, so a match at many independent loci is strong evidence, though no match probability is exactly zero."
 b. Example E.2 steps: "This is about twice the human population (~8 billion), so a random match at all 6 loci is extremely unlikely, although not strictly impossible." With 8 billion people the expected number of matching people is 8 × 10^9 ÷ 1.56 × 10^10 ≈ 0.5, so a match somewhere in the world is not unlikely; for one named random person it is 1 in 15.6 billion. Fix [NEW]: "This is about twice the human population (~8 billion), so a randomly chosen person is very unlikely to match at all 6 loci, although among all 8 billion people about one half of a match would be expected, so six loci alone could not prove identity." Arithmetic 50^6 = 1.5625 × 10^10, 1/ that = 6.4 × 10^-11: correct.
 Kind: unclear or misleading. Source: book. Likely.

8. **C, Example C.2 and practice C.3: nucleotide counts** (p. 278). Possible to likely.

## Overall judgement
The transcription of Additional topics A to G and their key points is faithful and complete; I found no slip. The errors are the book's. The most serious are the false "chapter never covers X" introductions (every one of the shared introduction, A, B, C, D, E carries at least one), the wrong "degenerate and unambiguous are opposites" trap, the wrong "higher than in (a)" in C practice 5, the misleading random-match and "essentially certain" statements, the swapped-children item without a premise, and several unqualified biology lines (Chargaff, polymerase I, poly-A after termination, Y-STRs unchanged, "unique"). Arithmetic is sound throughout; the number problems are the 30,000 genes, 27,000 bp gene and 3.2 billion differing from NCERT. The key points are the most accurate part.
