## Suspected book errors (page, exact words, why, correction, confidence)
1. p. 561, Pro-Tip: "If a question says “antigen–antibody,” the answer is ELISA, every time." Overgeneral: Western blot, immunofluorescence, agglutination and radioimmunoassay also rest on antigen–antibody binding. Add "among the techniques of this chapter". Confidence high it is overgeneral; medium that it matters for NEET.
2. p. 561, Pitfall 1: "This is the single most-tested probe error." Unsupported superlative; labelled; suggest dropping the sentence. High.
3. p. 558, 4.3.4: "(for example, in HIV screening and pregnancy test kits)". Home pregnancy kits are lateral-flow immunoassays, not ELISA (hospital hCG ELISA exists). Suggest "HIV screening" only, or "immunoassay-based kits". Medium.
4. p. 557, 4.3.2 and p. 556: PCR "makes millions of copies of a pathogen’s nucleic acid" for HIV (an RNA virus) and "DNA/RNA": PCR amplifies DNA; RNA first needs reverse transcription (RT-PCR). NCERT only says PCR detects HIV; add "(RT-PCR for RNA)". Medium.
5. p. 556 (4.2 table) "Enzyme-Linked Immuno Sorbent Assay" against p. 575 "enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay": inconsistent spelling; use "immunosorbent" (NCERT writes "Immuno-sorbent"). Low.
6. p. 563 (Use 4 table row: "Test vaccines (e.g., polio vaccine) on transgenic mice before human use, replacing monkeys"), p. 566 (Practice 5 answer: "transgenic mice are used to test the polio vaccine before human use"; MCQ 6 "Transgenic mice are used to test the polio vaccine"). Overstated against NCERT, whose wording the book itself copies on p. 563 ("are being used ... and could replace"): NCERT says the mice are being developed and, if reliable, could replace monkeys. Change "replacing" to "that could replace", "are used" to "are being developed to test". Medium-high.
7. p. 562 and throughout: "Rosie ... the first transgenic cow (1997)". NCERT gives no year for Rosie; I could not confirm 1997 (Rosie's milk was reported in the late 1990s; the first transgenic bovine, Herman the bull, dates from 1990, which fits the book's "cow" wording and MCQ 5 (d) calling Herman "a distractor", but a student could dispute it). Keep (the brief says keep and report); the 1997 and "first" are unsupported in the book. Low confidence.
8. p. 562 "about 95% of all existing transgenic animals are mice": NCERT says "more than 95 per cent". Cheat sheet and MCQ 1 print "about"/"~"; consistent with the book, fine. Low.
9. p. 564 "Whole animals are also irreplaceable for studying a disease" and p. 562 "priceless": overgeneral wording (cell and organoid models exist). Low.
10. p. 568 to 573, GEAC: the body's official name is "Genetic Engineering Appraisal Committee" (MoEFCC); the book (and older NCERT) say "Approval", teaches it as the only form (Pitfall 2 "learn it exactly", MCQ 1 distractors). Copy; note both. Confidence medium-high on the official name; NEET answers "Approval" per NCERT.
11. p. 570, 569, 573: "the Indian Patents Bill and related amendments". The Bill passed long ago as an Act (Patents (Second Amendment) Act, 2002, with later amendments); NCERT's "recently cleared" is dated. Suggest "Indian Patents Bill (now the Patents Act, as amended)". Medium.
12. p. 570, 6.3.4: Neem "faced patenting", Turmeric "patented by a US university", Basmati "a foreign company patented a rice variety". No company, year or outcome is given (the book adds nothing unsupported); omitting that the turmeric and neem patents were revoked after Indian challenges leaves the impression they stood. Low-medium; add nothing without the founder's call.
13. p. 570, 6.3.5 and p. 573 MCQ 5: the "three faces of biopiracy" (intellectual, resource, economic piracy) are not NCERT terms and the book cites no source, yet MCQ 5 tests them. Report; suggest marking "(the book’s own framing, not NCERT)" or dropping MCQ 5. Medium.
14. p. 569 6.3.3 and p. 573 MCQ 6: "(a), (b), (c) are each individually patentable" (GM plant strain, DNA sequence and encoded protein, process). Jurisdiction-dependent: the Indian Patents Act (s. 3(j), 3(c)) excludes plants and animals in whole or part and the mere discovery of a living thing; US law (Myriad, 2013) rejected patents on isolated natural DNA. Qualify to "may be patentable". Medium-high.
15. p. 573, Pitfall 1: "Examiners swap these in distractors constantly." Unsupported frequency; labelled. High.
16. p. 574 to 575, ELISA protocol: step 1 says "The antigen (or antibody) of known identity is adsorbed" and step 3 "The patient sample is added", which is the indirect/sandwich layout; the next paragraph says of direct ELISA "the antigen (from the sample) is adsorbed to the well". The "standard four-step protocol" does not fit the direct format it is meant to cover. Add "(indirect format)" to the protocol or state the direct variation. Medium.
17. p. 575 table: indirect ELISA "Target detected: Antibody", direct "Antigen". Indirect ELISA is also used to detect antigens (its defining feature is the enzyme on a secondary antibody). "Usually" is missing. Medium-low.
18. p. 575, Detection step: "The reaction is stopped with acid". HRP/TMB is stopped with acid (so the blue turns yellow, which the book says), but alkaline phosphatase/p-nitrophenyl phosphate is usually stopped with NaOH, and HRP also needs hydrogen peroxide (not named). Say "an acid or base as the enzyme requires". Medium-low.
19. p. 577, Answer 6 (coloured vs colourless substrate): the reasoning ("would give colour whether or not the enzyme was present") assumes a coloured substrate that does not change colour; a substrate that changes colour would also report. The usual reason is low background and a clear signal. Low.
20. p. 576, Example A.3: "one primary antibody can bind multiple enzyme-linked secondary antibodies" is right as written (several secondaries bind one primary), no change; noted because a student may read it as a primary binding the secondaries.
21. p. 556, "usually one per paper", p. 562 "perennial", p. 568 "Almost always": exam frequencies (labelled).
